A **time signature** is two numbers stacked on top of each other doing all the talking: top for how many, bottom for of what. That's the whole contract, and somehow entire semesters get built defending it.

Then the shapes doing the counting: a **whole note** eats a whole measure, a **half note** eats half, a **quarter note** a quarter, an **eighth note** an eighth, a **sixteenth note** a sixteenth, on down through **32nd** and **64th notes** — each one twice as fast as the last, a doubling ladder with no plateau. **Rests** are that same ladder, just quiet about it.

**Dots and ties** run their own hustle. A **tie** links two notes of the *same* pitch into one longer note; a **slur** does connected phrasing across *different* pitches — a similar curve, a completely different job, and half of music notation's confusion lives in that distinction. A **dot** adds half the note's own value back onto itself; **double-dotted notes** stack a second dot worth a quarter of the original — diminishing returns, like everything else that compounds.

Underneath all of it sits the **beat**, the pulse you'd tap a foot to, a.k.a. the **pulse**, and how fast it goes is the **tempo** — Allegro, Andante, or just a number if you're not feeling poetic. **Meter** is how many beats live in a **measure** (the **bar**) and how those beats split. **Duple**, **triple**, **quadruple** meter: two, three, or four beats a bar. **Simple meter** splits each beat into two; **compound meter** splits it into three, which is what a time signature with 6, 9, or 12 on top is quietly telling you.

And when a beat gets greedy, a **tuplet** crams notes where they don't belong: a **triplet** fits three notes where two normally live, a **quintuplet** fits five, a **sextuplet** six, a **septuplet** seven. Compound meter runs the trick backward — a **duplet** squeezes two notes into a dotted note's space, a **quadruplet** squeezes four.
