A **third** and a **fifth** are the two intervals that build almost all of Western harmony. An interval's number counts letter names, both ends included: C up to E passes through C–D–E, three letters, so it is a third; C up to G passes through five letters, so it is a fifth. The number gives the size on the staff; the [[semitone]] count gives the quality, minor or major for thirds, perfect for the fifth.

**Table 1 — Thirds and fifths above C.** *How each is counted in letters and semitones, and the simple ratio it is tuned toward.*
| Interval | Example | Letters | Semitones | Pure ratio |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Minor third | C to E♭ | 3 | 3 | 6:5 |
| Major third | C to E | 3 | 4 | 5:4 |
| Diminished fifth | C to G♭ | 5 | 6 | — |
| Perfect fifth | C to G | 5 | 7 | 3:2 |
| Augmented fifth | C to G♯ | 5 | 8 | — |

Stack a third and a fifth on any note and you have a [[triad]]: the fifth is the sturdy frame, the third decides the color, major or minor (see [[chord_quality]]). The fifth is the strongest consonance after the octave, the 2/3 point of the [[monochord]] string, and twelve of them in a row are what opens the [[pythagorean_comma]] (see [[why_12_notes]]). The third is fussier: four pure fifths stacked give a harsh, wide major third, the "Pythagorean third" of [[pythagorean_tuning]], and [[meantone]] was invented to soften it. On a guitar, [[the_fret_problem]] shows why the equal-tempered major third sounds about 14 cents sharp of the pure 5:4.
