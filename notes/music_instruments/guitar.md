Standard tuning sets the six open strings to **E2 A2 D3 G3 B3 E4** — the same pitch classes ([[pitch_class]]) as any other instrument, just fixed as starting points a few [[semitone|semitones]] apart (four, between the G and B strings; five, everywhere else). Moving up one fret raises a string by exactly one semitone, so once you know where a note is on one string, the rest follow from counting.

Twelve frets is exactly one [[octave]]: fret 12 on any string is the same [[pitch_class]] as that string open, one octave higher. The pattern then repeats. Every note on the fretboard, open string through fret 12:

Notice the same [[pitch_class]] shows up in several places at once — e.g. the open 2nd string (B3) and fret 4 of the 3rd string are both B, just reached differently. That's the thing a guitar has that a piano doesn't: [[piano_note_names|a piano has exactly one key per pitch class per octave]]; a guitar reaches most pitch classes on more than one string.

## History

The word "guitar" comes from the Spanish *guitarra*. By 1200 Spain had two instruments with that name, the *guitarra latina* and the *guitarra morisca*, and the Spanish vihuela of the 15th and 16th centuries was a major influence. The five-course baroque guitar appeared in mid-16th-century Spain and stayed popular in Spain, Italy and France into the mid-18th century. Placing the frets was a tuning problem from the start — see [[rule_of_eighteen]].

Around 1850 Spanish makers, notably Antonio de Torres, settled the modern form; Torres enlarged the body and introduced fan bracing, which made the guitar much louder. The electric guitar followed: the first were patented in 1937, and solid-body designs came from Gibson (with Les Paul) and Leo Fender after the Second World War.
